I recently saw Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey.  I have thoughts.

Ignoring the advice I often dispense in situations like these, I decided to
read the poem beforehand.  I originally bought a cheap, used prose
translation by W. H. D. Rouse that I found difficult to get through, so I
pivoted early on to Emily Wilson's version and found it *much* more
accessible.  I'll probably have much more to say about the experience of
reading the Odyssey, particularly since I was in the perhaps unusual
position, for an amateur, of having two translations at my fingertips at the
same time, but that is a discussion for another day.  Suffice it to say I
read the poem before the movie and it unsurprisingly coloured my experience
of the whole thing.

Let's get this out of the way first.  I really liked the movie, but I didn't
*love* it.  I thought it looked amazing and found myself genuinely on the
edge of my seat during certain sequences, like the famous Circe scene
(*shudder*).  The Sirens were handled *perfectly*.  I loved how Agamemnon is
portrayed as a kind of towering, larger than life, yet mysterious figure who
casts a shadow over the whole movie (Nolan's decision to show only glimpses
of his face was genius.  He's like the ancient Greek version of Darth
Vader).  And the Trojan Horse itself felt - I never thought I would say
this - *fresh*.

With that said, Nolan made changes from the source material, and I didn't
always feel like it was for the better.  Please don't misunderstand me - I
don't think that faithfulness to source material is the metric by which we
should judge adaptations - particularly not the adaptation of a 3000 year
poem, itself likely an amalgamation of different versions of the mythology.
It's kind of crazy to talk about narrative purity here.  Book Odysseus and
film Odysseus are two different characters and that is not, in and of
itself, a bad thing.

Except, of course, that certain story beats only make sense when we're
talking about one version of the character over the other, and therein lies
part of the problem.  Film Odysseus is apparently the kind of guy who plucks
his bow before shooting an animal he's hunting to give it a fighting chance.
It seems strange that a guy like that would fire a random arrow at a
Cyclops, as they are making a successful getaway, just to be mean. This
happens in the poem, and poem Odysseus can be kind of a dick, so it actually
tracks, but it felt out of character for film Odysseus.  I'm not sure why
Nolan bothered to keep it in.

Speaking of which, book Odysseus is supposed to be clever and wily.  Film
Odysseus is just a tortured soldier with PTSD.  Technically, this isn't
actually that big of a problem in the movie because Nolan restructures the
plot enough so that Odysseus' cleverness isn't actually required to move the
plot forward.  So it mostly works in the movie - I guess just missed seeing
the wily version Odysseus make an appearance (I suspect that NOT reading the
poem beforehand would have helped here).  The movie barely mentions that
he's the one that came up with the idea for the Trojan Horse.  It omits the
memorable scene with Odysseus and his men clinging to the undersides of the
Cyclops' sheep.  It omits the famous "Nobody" ruse.  Again - it more or less
works in the movie - I was just disappointed.

## Omitting Phaeacia

All in all, Nolan seemed to be aiming for a more grounded approach to the
source material.  The Greek pantheon forms a major part of the poem -
Athena, Zeus, Hermes and Poseidon all figure quite heavily in the plot.
Nolan more or less leached them all out the film, except for Athena - and
even she was a bit of a psych (if you know, you know).  Zeus and Poseidon
basically just show up as bad weather.

Which makes it all the more odd that Nolan decided to omit the whole
Phaeacia episode of the story.  In the book, after leaving Calypso but
before returning to Ithaca, Odysseus crash lands on an island of seafarers
called Phaeacia, where he's forced to be their guest while waiting for a
chance to leave.  Some of the most famous elements of the story, like the
Cyclops and Circe and the Sirens, essentially unfold as a flashback at this
point - a tale spun by Odysseus to entertain his hosts.  It's ambiguous as
to how much of it is actually real.

Nolan got rid of this whole section, and instead folded it into Odysseus'
time with Calypso, now retooled so that Odysseus is an amnesiac.  The
flashback, where we meet the Cyclops, etc. is reimagined as Odysseus
regaining his lost memories.

To me, this undermines the whole idea of "grounding" the narrative.  The
fantastical elements of the story are effectively portrayed as actual
memories in the film - real in a way that they are not in the poem.  It's
frustrating because if you were looking for a way to ground the story, the
poem's flashback already seemed like a perfectly good way of doing so.  As I
said before, the poem already leaves it ambiguous as to how much of
Odysseus' tale reflects reality, or just his own ego.  It was just strange
that Nolan would choose to purge the gods and goddesses from his movie - but
keep the Cyclops, Sirens, and witches who turn men into pigs.

## Unimportant Details

Much has been made of Nolan's other choices for the film, like casting a
black actress as Helen of Troy, and his use of modern language.  I debated
even mentioning them, for fear of giving undeserved oxygen to assholes who
think that "historical accuracy" applies to a fucking *myth*, but I finally
concluded that either a) no one is going is to read this anyway or b) people
*will* read it and it's an oppourtunity to call these people out.

With respect to the use of modern words like "dad", you get used to it
really quickly.  I always marvel at people who complain about this.
Anything other than ancient Greek is going to be "wrong" so if you're doing
the film in English anyway, you might as well use words that seem
appropriate.

And once you accept that the film is a more modern take on this story, the
diversity of the cast doesn't feel out of place in the slightest.  I would
take it even further: the scenes with Helen and Clytemnestra are just
*really good* - probably some of the best scenes of the entire film.  I'll
credit those scenes with making me actually want to read the Oresteia.
